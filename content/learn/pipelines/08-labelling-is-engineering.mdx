## 1. A guideline is the actual deliverable

The instinct when a dataset needs labels is to write a one-line definition for each label and hand it to whoever's free. "Helpful: the response answers the question. Unhelpful: it doesn't." That sentence feels complete right up until two people apply it to the same ambiguous example and land on opposite answers, because the sentence never told them what to do with the case that's mostly helpful but skips a caveat, or technically correct but useless.

The examples that actually need a guideline are the ones near the boundary, and a guideline that only states the rule - without showing how the rule resolves the hard cases - hasn't done its job yet. A guideline worth using reads more like a short reference document: the rule, three or four worked examples chosen specifically because they're borderline, and the reasoning for why each one landed where it did. That document is not a memo you write once and forget. It's the actual specification for what your dataset means, and it should be versioned and updated the same way you'd version code - because the first round of labelling will surface cases the guideline didn't anticipate, and the fix belongs in the guideline, not just in one labeller's head.

Treat the first day of any labelling effort as a calibration session rather than production output. Have everyone label the same small batch, then sit down together and walk through every disagreement out loud. Most of what surfaces in that conversation isn't someone being careless - it's a genuine ambiguity nobody wrote down, and the fix is a sentence added to the guideline, not a note to "be more careful next time." Skipping this step is the single most common reason a set ends up like the one this lesson opened on.

## 2. Measure agreement - don't assume it

The opening scene of this lesson is what happens when nobody checks. The fix is not complicated: before trusting a full labelling pass, have two people independently label a shared subset - a few dozen examples is usually enough to see a real signal - and measure how often they agree.

Raw percent agreement is the obvious first number, and it's the wrong one to stop at, because it doesn't account for how often two labellers would agree purely by chance. If 90% of examples are the common label, two labellers who each just guess the common label most of the time will "agree" 80%+ of the time without paying attention. Cohen's kappa corrects for that by comparing observed agreement against chance agreement.

| Metric | What it tells you | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Raw percent agreement | How often the two labels matched | Inflated by an imbalanced label distribution |
| Cohen's kappa | Agreement beyond what chance alone predicts | Can look artificially low on imbalanced data - the "kappa paradox" |
| Weighted kappa | Same idea, for ordinal scales (a 1-5 rating) where near-misses matter less than opposite ends | Needs the label scale to actually be ordered |

The practical habit: compute both, and read them together. A kappa of 0.45 alongside 60% raw agreement on a two-class, roughly balanced label is a real problem worth fixing before labelling continues. The same kappa alongside 95% raw agreement on a heavily imbalanced label is often the paradox showing up, not a real quality problem - which is exactly why reporting kappa alone, without the raw number next to it, misleads more than it informs.

```python
from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score

labeller_a = ["helpful", "unhelpful", "helpful", "helpful", "unhelpful"]
labeller_b = ["helpful", "helpful",   "helpful", "unhelpful", "unhelpful"]

kappa = cohen_kappa_score(labeller_a, labeller_b)
agreement = sum(a == b for a, b in zip(labeller_a, labeller_b)) / len(labeller_a)

print(f"raw agreement: {agreement:.2f}   kappa: {kappa:.2f}")
# report both - kappa alone, on a small or imbalanced sample, is easy to misread
```

> Agreement you haven't measured isn't agreement. It's luck you haven't tested yet.

When the two labellers disagree on a specific example, resist the urge to just take a majority vote and move on - that hides the disagreement instead of learning from it. Send disputed cases to a third person acting as adjudicator, whose job includes asking whether the guideline itself has a gap the disagreement just exposed. Half the value of adjudication is fixing the guideline, not just picking a winner.

Do this measurement again whenever anything changes - a new labeller joins, the guideline gets revised, or the source data shifts in some way you can feel but haven't quantified. Agreement measured once at the start and never again is a snapshot, not a guarantee, and the set that quietly drifts out of agreement over months is far harder to notice than the one that was wrong from day one.

## 3. Treat the queue as infrastructure, not a spreadsheet

A spreadsheet filled in once, by whoever was available, has no mechanism for catching drift, fatigue, or a labeller who quietly started interpreting the guideline differently in week three. An annotation queue that's actually built as infrastructure has a few features a spreadsheet doesn't: it can mix in gold-standard examples - items with a known correct label - so you get an ongoing accuracy check on every labeller without needing a separate audit; it can route a configurable percentage of items to two labellers instead of one, so agreement is measured continuously rather than as a one-time exercise before the "real" labelling starts; and it can track which items were adjudicated and why, so guideline gaps get found instead of quietly re-occurring.

None of this requires exotic tooling - a queue like this can be a table with a status column and a scheduled job, or a dedicated annotation platform if the volume justifies it. What matters is the shape: labelling is a running process with quality checks built into its normal operation, not an event that happens once and is trusted forever afterward. And when the guideline changes - because it will, the moment a new edge case shows up - re-run the agreement check. A guideline that was reliable in January doesn't stay reliable through a March revision without being tested again; treat it exactly like the code it effectively is.

The team in this lesson's opening scene didn't fail because their labellers were careless. They failed because nobody treated the guideline as something that could be wrong, and nobody built a way to find out before the set was already in use. Both are cheap to fix ahead of time and expensive to fix after a decision has already been made on top of the bad third.
